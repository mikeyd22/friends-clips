# Research Foundations for Viral Clip Selection

Use research to constrain claims, not to create a universal formula for reach. Content controls only part of a system that also includes audience, packaging, distribution, and social feedback.

## Supported operational implications

- Sharing can provide self-related and social value: helping someone, representing identity, strengthening a relationship, or signaling useful knowledge.
- Practical utility, interest, surprise, positivity, and some content-elicited emotions can be associated with sharing, but findings vary by content type, audience, and platform.
- A bounded information gap can sustain attention when the viewer understands what is missing and expects an answer inside the clip.
- The imagined recipient matters: public broadcasting and one-to-one sending can involve different social motives.
- Low-to-moderate controversy may invite conversation, while high social cost can suppress transmission.
- Platform watch and interaction signals make early clarity, progression, payoff, and send value reasonable mechanisms to test; they do not establish fixed hook, duration, completion, or share thresholds.

## What the evidence does not establish

- Transcript quality alone cannot reliably predict a hit.
- Views alone do not prove that a particular edit caused the result.
- A viral example does not prove a reusable causal formula.
- There is no universal ideal duration, mandatory hook formula, fixed completion threshold, or guaranteed algorithmic boost.
- More outrage, faster speech, more cuts, or removing every pause does not automatically improve performance.

## Transfer rule

When applying evidence, state whether the support is Direct, Adjacent, Local, or Heuristic. Record the population, platform, content type, and year before transferring a finding. Treat accuracy, context, privacy, safety, and reputational integrity as gates rather than tradeable engagement points.
